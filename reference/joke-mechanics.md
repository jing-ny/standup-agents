# Joke mechanics — the panel's vocabulary, decoded

The panel's verdicts name mechanisms. This card is what those names mean —
one definition, one spotting cue, one tiny synthetic example each. It is not
a course in comedy writing; it's a decoder ring for your feedback.
(Framework debts: structure ideas after Judy Carter's *Comedy Bible*;
truth+pain after John Vorhaus's *The Comic Toolbox*. The words below are ours.)

## Premise

**What:** the absurd truth a bit exists to expose — the one-sentence claim
under all its jokes. Setups and punchlines come and go; the premise is what
they're all proving.
**Spot it:** can you finish "the funny thing nobody says out loud is …" for
this bit? If not, you have jokes in search of a bit.
**Example:** "IKEA furniture isn't a product, it's a psychological evaluation
you pay to take."

## Setup / punchline

**What:** the setup builds an expectation; the punchline breaks it. Premise →
setup → punchline, that order, no exceptions hiding in "storytelling."
**Spot it:** can you draw a line in the beat where the audience's guess forms?
If there's no guess, there's nothing to break.
**Example:** "My gym has a sauna, a pool, and a juice bar." (guess forming:
fancy gym) "I've seen none of them. I go for the parking." (guess broken.)

## Punch word

**What:** the word that carries the surprise — and it belongs at the end of
the sentence. Everything after it is applause you're talking over.
**Spot it:** say the line, find the laugh-word, check what follows it.
**Example:** weak: "There's a raccoon in my kitchen every morning, which is
stressful." strong: "Every morning, my kitchen has a raccoon."

## Misdirect → trapdoor

**What:** the setup points the audience confidently down one path; the punch
opens a floor they didn't know was there. The laugh is the drop.
**Spot it:** would a stranger finish your setup the wrong way? Good — that's
the misdirect working. If they'd finish it *your* way, the trapdoor is
painted on.
**Example:** "My landlord finally fixed the heat." (path: gratitude) "Now I
can hear the mice being comfortable."

## Joke vs observation

**What:** the panel's funny-first veto. An observation is true and clever; a
joke has a trapdoor. Rooms nod at observations. Nodding is silence with
manners.
**Spot it:** where exactly does the laugh come *out loud*? If the honest
answer is "it's more of a vibe," it's an observation.
**Example:** observation: "Self-checkout machines make customers do the
staff's job." joke: "I love self-checkout. I steal one grape every time.
Not for the grape — for the win."

## Act-out vs narration

**What:** narration reports a scene; an act-out performs it — voice, body,
present tense. One act-out is worth three descriptions of the same moment.
**Spot it:** are you *telling* the audience someone said a thing, or *being*
the person saying it?
**Example:** narration: "My dentist kept making small talk during the
drilling." act-out: [drill whine] "'So! Any fun travel plans?'"

## Rule of three

**What:** two items build a pattern, the third breaks it. Two isn't a pattern;
four is a list.
**Spot it:** count the items; check the break lands on the last one.
**Example:** "I left the party early because I was tired, I had work in the
morning, and the host asked who invited me."

## Callback

**What:** a planted word or image from early material pays off later, with
new information. A callback that just repeats the joke is a rerun.
**Spot it:** does the payoff *recast* the planted thing, or re-tell it?
**Example:** plant: the one grape. Twenty minutes later, closing a bit about
your wedding: "Open bar, live band — and in my pocket, one grape. For the win."

## Tag

**What:** a bonus punch stacked on a laugh that's already happening — same
setup, no new one needed.
**Spot it:** it only works while they're still laughing. If it needs its own
setup, it's the next joke.
**Example:** "…for the win. [laugh] I'm banned from two Krogers. [tag]"

## Save line

**What:** a pre-planned line for when a planned laugh doesn't come. Written
by you, in your voice, before you need it — because on stage you won't have it.
**Spot it (in a set file):** every risky beat should have one nearby; the
panel flags where one is missing (and never writes it — that's yours).
**Example shape:** acknowledging the silence beats explaining the joke.
"Okay, that one's just for me."

## Commitment vs fact exaggeration

**What:** exaggerating *how much you care* scales a joke up; exaggerating
*the facts* usually just breaks belief.
**Spot it:** does the bigger version make you more ridiculous, or the story
less believable?
**Example:** fact (weak): "My dog eats a thousand dollars of food a month."
commitment (strong): "My dog eats better than me. I plan my meals around his."

## Specific beats universal

**What:** "a 2004 Corolla" is funnier than "an old car" — specificity does
the imagining for the audience.
**Spot it:** hunt your draft for the words *thing, stuff, someone, a place* —
each is a specificity refund waiting to be claimed.
**Example:** "I got him a gift" → "I got him a Costco membership. Executive tier."
